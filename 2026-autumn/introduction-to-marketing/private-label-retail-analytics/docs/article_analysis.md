# Article analysis — Begley & McOuat (2020)

*Turning Private Labels into Powerhouse Brands*, McKinsey & Company, October 2020.
This document is the analytical basis for the Part C reflection in the report.

## Central argument

Private labels are "having a moment": during COVID-19, availability gaps and price
pressure pushed US consumers toward store brands (nearly 40% tried new brands; ~19%
bought more private label; price was the #1 reason for >45% of switchers, availability
#2 — Exhibits 1–2, pp. 2–3). But the boom is fragile. Most retailers never designed
their private labels to build loyalty — products often exist simply because a vendor
offered cheap supply, without a defined value proposition (p. 4). Unless retailers
"step up their private-label game", consumers will switch back to national brands
post-crisis. The opportunity: convert short-term switching into long-term store
loyalty by managing private labels as real brands.

## The five strategic areas (pp. 4–9)

1. **Brand strategy** — define, per brand and sub-brand, who the target customer is and
   what distinctive need the value proposition meets; set guardrails so the proposition
   is executed consistently. Consumers typically recognise private labels as *cheaper*,
   not as *better*.
2. **Assortment and pricing** — rationalise overlapping SKUs, find white-space
   opportunities (pack sizes, flavours), and systematically enforce **price gapping**:
   each private-label SKU benchmarked against a reference national brand. Promotions on
   national brands erode the private-label value story if gaps are not maintained.
3. **Communication, marketing and packaging** — historically private labels relied on
   price and shelf position. Leading retailers now close the quality-perception gap
   through storytelling (sourcing, origin), third-party quality certification, and
   packaging that conveys functional benefits rather than imitating national brands.
4. **Product design and sourcing** — avoid the "price back" sourcing mistake (setting
   margin-rate targets that destroy penny profit); use consumer-driven specifications
   and total-cost KPIs; innovate where the private label can lead the category.
5. **Organisation and operating model** — no one-size-fits-all: centralised teams matter
   most early on and in mature markets; lean central guardrails + category-team
   execution suit price-focused markets.

## The three-stage maturity model (p. 5)

| Stage | Markets (examples) | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Optimisation of core assortment | Brazil, Colombia, Mexico | Opening price points, me-too equivalents of national brands; drives margin and price perception, **not** loyalty |
| 2. Private-label portfolio management | Japan, United States | Additional brands/sub-brands covering wider need states (premium, organic, healthy); pricing and promotional guardrails across tiers |
| 3. Sophisticated private-label ownership | Canada, Germany, Spain, UK | CPG-like brand management; innovation, storytelling, stand-alone loyalty that rivals national brands as a reason to switch stores |

## Critical assessment

- **Context-bound evidence.** The consumer statistics are from US grocery surveys run
  March–September 2020 (n ≈ 1,500–2,000 per wave), mid-pandemic. Panic buying and stock
  shortages are unusual switching drivers; the 40%-tried-something-new figure should not
  be read as normal-market behaviour, and must not be quoted as 2026 Finnish data.
- **Direction still valid, mechanism caution.** The strategic logic — switching is easy,
  loyalty must be earned through quality and differentiation — is not date-stamped. But
  the article conflates *trial* (availability/price-driven) with *loyalty*
  (quality/identity-driven); its own Exhibit 2 shows 26% of switchers bought private
  label *only* because the national brand was unavailable.
- **Finland 2026 fit.** Finland resembles the stage-2/stage-3 profile the article
  attributes to mature European markets: tiered private-label architectures (value →
  mainstream → premium), own-brand quality certification, and loyalty-programme
  integration (Plussa, S-Etukortti) give Finnish retailers instruments the 2020 US
  market largely lacked. The framework is usable, but the Finnish market is arguably
  *ahead* of the article's baseline assumptions.

## Connection to our findings

- **Assortment and pricing / price gapping** is directly testable with our data: the
  23–33% unit-price gaps we measured (Pirkka, Xtra, Coop vs Taffel) are exactly the
  "price gap" discipline the article describes. Xtra fits stage 1 (opening price point);
  Pirkka and Coop fit stage 2 (mainstream me-too with quality communication).
- **Brand strategy**: Kesko's tiered architecture (K-Menu value / Pirkka mainstream /
  Pirkka Parhaat premium) is a textbook stage-2→3 portfolio; S Group's multi-brand
  portfolio (Xtra value, Coop mainstream, Kotimaista origin-positioned) maps onto the
  "brands and sub-brands to cover wider range of customer-need states" description.
  Lidl's 150+ private labels (KKV 3/2025) with no national-brand benchmark in our sample
  is consistent with the article's "retailers that primarily carry private labels"
  loyalty play (p. 8).
- **What our data cannot test**: organisation/operating model (internal), product
  design and sourcing (supplier relationships), and actual consumer loyalty. We see the
  *outputs* of strategy (prices, assortment, brand architecture), not the strategy
  process itself.
