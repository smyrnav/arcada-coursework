# Narration script — Private labels are more than a cheaper option

Individual presentation, Introduction to Marketing 2026. Approximately 4–4.5 minutes at a natural pace. The same text is embedded in the PowerPoint speaker notes. This is a reflection on the research — the full data, tables and charts are in the submitted report.

---

**Slide 1 — Cover (~15 s)**

Hi, I'm Smyrna. This presentation is not a repeat of my report — the numbers are all in there if you want them. What I want to do here is tell you what doing this research actually made me think about: what surprised me, what changed my mind, and what I'm still wondering about private labels in Finnish retail.

**Slide 2 — What I set out to understand (~25 s)**

My starting question was simple: how do Finnish retailers use their own brands, and what can product data tell me about why customers choose them. I researched it digitally — retailer catalogues, delivery menus and published consumer research, all analysed in Python. And one honesty note up front: I didn't visit stores or observe customers. That limitation turned out to be one of the most interesting parts of the whole project, so keep it in mind.

**Slide 3 — Three findings I did not expect (~40 s)**

Three things genuinely surprised me. First, the price gaps: the own-brand crisps I sampled were 23 to 33 percent cheaper per kilo than the matched Taffel products. That's a big gap — and it immediately raises the question of why everyone doesn't just buy the cheaper one. Second, the identical kiosk menus: I was sure a station kiosk and a neighbourhood kiosk would sell different things. Same six products, same prices. My segmentation assumption was too easy. And third, the baguette: same weight, same filling, 37.5 percent more at the kiosk. That told me location and immediacy are literally part of the price.

**Slide 4 — The number that changed my question (~35 s)**

This is the number that stayed with me. Between 2016 and 2023, private labels held roughly a quarter of Finnish food sales value — and that share barely moved, even though retailers kept adding more and more own-brand products. More products, same share. That's when my question changed. It stopped being about which product is cheaper, and became about what makes people come back. The McKinsey article gave me the vocabulary: price or availability can trigger a trial, but trial is not loyalty. And honestly, that reframed everything else I had found.

**Slide 5 — What a price comparison cannot tell me (~30 s)**

Doing this digitally taught me something about research itself. A catalogue gives me prices and pack sizes, but not people. A promotion can flip a price comparison overnight — I literally saw Taffel multi-buy offers sitting next to regular-price own brands. An online listing is not a shelf, and a delivery menu is not a store. So my method is good at answering "what" and "how much", and genuinely bad at "who" and "why". And the why is the interesting part. I'd rather tell you that honestly than pretend my data says more than it does.

**Slide 6 — It is not only about price (~35 s)**

So if price gaps of 30 percent don't automatically win customers, what does? The PTT survey gave me part of the answer. Yes, most people see private labels as cheaper, and nearly half see the quality as equal. But 58 percent prefer a Finnish manufacturer brand over a foreign private label — and when both options are Finnish, preference actually flips toward the private label. Origin matters. And the Reddit thread showed me the same thing from the ground level: people judge products individually, and they check who actually makes them. One commenter had identified Valio behind a Pirkka cheese. Consumers do detective work. They don't buy "the cheap brand" — they buy products they trust.

**Slide 7 — What I noticed as a customer (~30 s)**

And then there's what I noticed simply by being a customer. Sello and Tripla are both R-kiosks, but they're built for completely different moments — a quick stop before the train versus a pass-through between platforms, where the renovation and the coffee station actually changed how the place feels. And a personal note: after almost three years in Finland, that blue and yellow has become a reassuring sight in grey weather — like spotting an inn through the snow, except the warm meal is a grilled-sausage hot dog. None of that is in my dataset. But it's exactly the kind of thing that makes someone choose a brand again.

**Slide 8 — What I learned from the research (~30 s)**

Four things I take with me. Segmentation: it's easy to invent customer groups from a location — the identical menus proved my own assumption wrong. Branding: the three retailers run genuinely different brand logics, and consumers still judge products one by one. Placement: I can describe the digital shelf honestly, but not the physical one. And methods: every source answers a different question, and the first mistake I had to unlearn was treating them as interchangeable.

**Slide 9 — The question I'm left with (~20 s)**

So that's where I landed. Price gets a product tried once. What gets it chosen again — trust, quality, origin, habit, even the feeling of a familiar blue-and-yellow kiosk — that's the question this project left me with, and it's the one I'd actually enjoy researching next. Thank you.
